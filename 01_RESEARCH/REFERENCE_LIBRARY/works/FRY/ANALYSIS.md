# FRY — strategic analysis

Source locator: physical PDF page numbers, 1-based, including front matter. English translation by Thomas Cleary, not evidence of either author's original Japanese syntax. Both books are instructional prose, largely direct address, definitions, contrasts, demonstrations and analogies; they are not narrative scenes. Beats below are changes in argument or recommended decision state, not claims that events occurred. All transfers are editorial interpretations, not empirical results.

## MUS-PREF | pp. 18–19
Musashi distinguishes his youthful victories from his later understanding. The initial record of wins gives way to uncertainty about why they happened, sustained practice and a more developed theory. Transfer: a favorable record cannot establish decision quality. Check alternative causes, including weaker opposition and variance. No fixed age or guaranteed progression imported.

## MUS-EARTH | pp. 20–30
Craft is first established as practical skill rather than display (20–21); the carpenter analogy distinguishes roles and material suitability (22–23); the five scrolls organize the argument (23–25); tool selection becomes conditional on setting (25–28); rhythm and discipline close the argument (28–30). Decision transition: attachment to a preferred instrument → inventory of available means → contextual allocation → repeated practice. TCG application: matchup-specific jobs, preparation, deck construction and reviewing decisions instead of prestige. Avoid attributing current deckbuilding theory to Musashi.

## MUS-WATER | pp. 31–49
The opening separates reading from practice; mental steadiness and broad observation (31–33) establish readiness. Formal positions and movement (34–40) provide a learnable baseline; flexible application (40–41) changes a fixed stance into a situational choice. Subsequent examples distinguish timing, purposeful action, and responding to changing conditions (41–48); the epilogue returns to long practice (49). Transfer: stable decision procedure, legal response windows, adaptability, and prioritization of public information. The physical examples remain historical, not instructions for real violence. No claim that a player reads a concealed card from posture.

## MUS-FIRE | pp. 50–64
The argument moves from practice and situation assessment (50–51) to initiative in several forms (52–53), commitment and opponent assessment (54–56), changing approach and testing concealed intentions (56–57), pressure and disruptions (58–61), abandoning repeated ineffective approaches and resetting perspective (61–64). Transfer: alternate lines, early interruption where legal, conditional probes, changing plans when evidence changes, and reassessment after a setback. No artificial intimidation, delay or misconduct imported; losing a board is not proof of losing composure. Each commitment needs an opportunity-cost and counterplay check.

## MUS-WIND | pp. 65–74
Musashi contrasts his approach with rival schools. He examines exaggerated reliance on a single tool or force (65–68), rigid positions and narrow attention (68–70), speed and movement (70–72), and the way instruction advances from accessible methods (72–74). Transfer: understand rival archetypes in their strongest context, avoid equating deck complexity or speed with skill, and teach through decision-relevant examples. The source is polemical; critiques are not neutral evidence that alternative schools or TCG decks are inferior.

## MUS-EMPTY | pp. 75–76
The argument distinguishes clarity developed through knowledge and practice from confusion mistaken for mastery. Transfer: fluent performance requires preparation and updating beliefs; intuition does not license inventing rules, reading minds or skipping difficult calculations. A protagonist can misread a situation even when experienced.

## YAG-KILLING | pp. 81–98
Yagyū widens the frame from a duel to responsibility and prevention (81–83), then distinguishes study from embodied competence (83–84). He separates outward manner from inward purpose, and anticipates response before it is committed (85–89). Timing and safe margins (89–93) lead into sustained attention and the hazards of fixation (93–98). Transfer: a player can establish a credible legal threat and let the opponent commit first; mentorship can preserve a healthy environment and independent agency. Tactical bluffing is restricted to permissible game decisions; do not misrepresent public state or use real-world coercion.

## YAG-LIFE | pp. 99–112
The text distinguishes concealed and manifested intentions (99–100); preparation and attention to what is available precede action (100–106). The author then treats hasty commitment and attention caught by a successful move (106–108), extending the critique of fixation to mental bias and interpretation (108–112). Transfer: build plausible hand ranges, define a contingency before committing, update after each public action, and separate emotional reaction from evidence. Concealment is uncertainty, not permission for omniscient POV. Ancient metaphysical and physiological accounts are not modern psychology.

## YAG-NOSWORD | pp. 113–119
The initial problem concerns functioning when a preferred means is unavailable (113–114); potential and its realized function depend on preparation (114–116); attention follows changing circumstances, without being held by a past event (116–118); the closing discussion examines attachment to judgments (118–119). Transfer: create a viable fallback plan, let different tools fulfill the same strategic objective, and revise confident evaluations. Freedom within a TCG still means obedience to its formal rules.

## Synthesis and boundaries
Musashi emphasizes deciding and sustaining an advantageous initiative; Yagyū often emphasizes inducing commitment, preparation and freedom from attachment. They overlap on perception, timing, practice, and adaptability. Use their contrast to produce legitimate disagreements between competitors, including competent reactive players, without declaring one school universally superior. Cross-domain inference is a hypothesis. Neither source is evidence for card effects, tournament policies, optimal plays, win rates, character history or neurological claims. Technical evidence and continuity gates supersede the analogies. Translator's front matter (10–16) and notes (77–78, 120–122) contextualize terminology; bibliography and promotional excerpt (123–136) excluded from transfer and counts. No extended quotations retained.
